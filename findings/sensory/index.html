Sensory and Cognitive Access

A tone at every floor, in a building full of people trying to concentrate.

The Sense level

Deep Accessibility asks whether a space works for the senses, not only the body (Deep Accessibility, 2013, as cited in La Monica, 2026, “Access and Accessibility” section, para. 3). The elevator tone helps people with vision loss, but it sounds at every floor in a study space and may disrupt people who need to concentrate or who have hyperacusis.

One group’s gain, another’s cost

Lid’s (2016, “Balancing democratic values and practical policy” section, para. 4) Oslo study shows the same tension: removing the gap at bus and tram stops helps wheelchair users but makes the correct stop harder to find for people with sight loss or cognitive disabilities. A feature that serves one group can cost another, as the elevator tone does.

Moritz et al. (2022, p. 196) report that cold rooms and inconsistent lighting affected students with mobility-related disabilities, so sensory conditions are not only an autism issue.

Input you cannot turn off

The course reading Autistic Access Needs: Notes on Accessibility asks us to look beyond physical barriers to how people experience a space. MacLennan et al. (2023, p. 416) found that autistic adults often describe environments as disabling or enabling, and point to sensory input people cannot control. The elevator tone is such an input: patrons cannot turn it off.

The animation Sensory Overload shows how everyday sounds, such as a phone ringing, a skateboard, clicking on a clipboard or coffee being poured, can overwhelm some autistic people when several inputs arrive at once (Alkurhah, 2015, 0:00–2:22). A tone at every floor is the same kind of small, repeated sound.

What does enable

Wide aisles and spaced furniture give room to move, and a librarian on every floor offers help. The library also has quiet study rooms and labs on every floor, built with glass and doors to block sound and placed at the corners. But they must be reserved and rarely open to walk-ins, so those who need quiet most may not get it when they arrive.

Key takeaway

The quiet space exists. The policy that governs it is what keeps it out of reach of the people most likely to need it without warning.
