What Was Overlooked

What the design considered, what it did not, and who got to decide.

Considered, and not

The design has clearly considered mobility on arrival and movement through the building, and less the sensory environment, washroom size and non-visible needs. Moritz et al. (2022, p. 193) show that non-visible disabilities carry a burden of disclosure and disbelief, and my checklist said little about them.

Distress is not always visible

Sensory Overload makes the same point from the inside: the person struggles while nobody around them can see it until they break down (Alkurhah, 2015, 1:37–1:44). Access therefore cannot depend on visible signs of distress.

Who decides what is “universal”

Jaulus (2025, pp. 10, 22) adds a question about who decides what counts as “universal,” since a feature that helps one person may hinder another.

Lid (2016, Conclusion section, para. 2) shows how this plays out: in the Oslo redesign, disability advocates sat on a reference group with little formal power, and the project documents did not reference the CRPD. Equal access, she concludes, means access to the decisions that shape a place as well as to the place itself.

Key takeaway

A building can meet several design principles and still be shaped entirely by people who will never use it the way its visitors do.
