Washrooms

A sign says who a room is for. It does not say whether the room fits them.

The space is not there

The washrooms do not meet Size and Space for Approach and Use. Access Now defines an accessible washroom as spacious, with grab bars and barrier-free sinks (AccessNow, n.d.).

A false promise

Slater and Jones (2021, p. 52) argue that an “accessible” label can make a false promise, because a sign says who a room is for, not whether it fits them. The listing’s tag and my observation disagree, an architectural barrier in the course’s terms (AODA, 2005, as cited in La Monica, 2026, “Barriers: What are those?” section, para. 1).

Why it matters here

A public library is the kind of free, shared facility where this matters most. Lid (2016), drawing on Nussbaum, argues that redesigning public space is essential to the dignity and self-respect of people with impairments, so a washroom only labelled accessible is a matter of equal citizenship, not convenience (Lid, 2016, “A spatial dimension of social justice” section, para. 2).

What the label should mean

A video demonstration of an accessible washroom shows what the label should mean: grab bars on more than one wall, an emergency call within reach, and a toilet paper dispenser a seated person can reach (Julie Sawchuk, 2024, 0:00–2:15). By comparison, the library’s washrooms are tight, making a mobility device hard to turn and leaving little room for anyone.

Key takeaway

This is the clearest failure in the building, and it is doubled by the listing: the room is too small, and the tag tells patrons otherwise.
