Recommendations

Five changes that answer the gaps above. Each one also helps people beyond disabled patrons.

1. Add or enlarge a universal washroom

Provide enough turning space for a mobility device, grab bars and barrier-free sinks, matching Access Now’s definition (AccessNow, n.d.) and Size and Space for Approach and Use.

Who else benefits: caregivers, parents with strollers, people carrying bags and anyone who needs extra room (Slater & Jones, 2021, p. 55). The demonstration video’s stall is a useful model, with grab bars on more than one wall and an emergency call and supplies within reach (Julie Sawchuk, 2024, 0:40–1:16).

2. Reduce the elevator tone’s disruption

Check whether the tone can be softened, and pair it with a visual floor indicator so no one depends on sound alone. This keeps the benefit for people with vision loss while easing the Sense-level problem (Deep Accessibility, 2013, as cited in La Monica, 2026, “Access and Accessibility” section, para. 3).

Who else benefits: students, staff and anyone on a quiet floor.

3. Keep some quiet rooms open to walk-ins

Set aside a few study rooms or a calm space that needs no reservation. This offers recovery from sensory overload without planning ahead (MacLennan et al., 2023, p. 418).

Who else benefits: autistic and other neurodivergent patrons, people with migraines, new parents and exam-week students.

4. Publish accessibility details online and update Access Now

List washroom sizes, elevator details, quiet spaces and a floor map so patrons can plan ahead. Predictability is a sensory and cognitive access need.

Who else benefits: newcomers, older adults and first-time visitors.

5. Involve disabled patrons in decisions

Invite disabled patrons to review changes before they are made, following Jaulus’s (2025, p. 32) relational design, and train staff on non-visible disabilities (Moritz et al., 2022, p. 200). Everyone then gets a library tested by people who use it differently.

Pereyra (2016, p. 159) notes that disabled people are the best experts on their own needs, so their input should be sought on lighting, ambient sound, floor width and table-and-chair designs. Lid (2016) adds that equal access includes access to decisions, so patrons need real influence, not only a seat on an advisory group. This follows “Nothing About Us Without Us,” which holds that no decisions about disabled people should be made without them (La Monica, 2026, “Accessible Canada Act” section, para. 3). Pereyra (2016, p. 175) names the same slogan and concludes that disabled people should be decision makers in research and design, not just recipients (p. 180).

Key takeaway

Four of these five changes cost little. The fifth, bringing patrons into the decision, is the one that would have caught the other four earlier.
