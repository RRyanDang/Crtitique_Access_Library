Key Terms

The vocabulary used throughout this report.

The social model of disability
A frame that locates exclusion in how spaces are built rather than in a person’s body (La Monica, 2026, “The Social Model of Disability” section, para. 5). A step that stops a wheelchair is a problem with the step.
Architectural, informational and policy barriers
The three kinds of barrier this report looks for (AODA, 2005, as cited in La Monica, 2026, “Barriers: What are those?” section, para. 1). A washroom too small to turn in is architectural; a listing that does not say so is informational; a rule that quiet rooms must be reserved is policy.
Accommodation versus accessibility
An accommodation is arranged on request, after the fact. Accessibility is built in, so nobody has to ask (La Monica, 2026, “Accommodation vs Accessibility” section, para. 3). Jaulus (2025, p. 26) argues that support should be proactive as well as reactive.
Deep Accessibility
A framework that asks whether a space works for the senses, not only the body (Deep Accessibility, 2013, as cited in La Monica, 2026, “Access and Accessibility” section, para. 3). Light, sound and temperature count as access conditions.
Non-visible disability
A disability that others cannot see. Moritz et al. (2022, p. 193) found that students with non-visible disabilities carry an extra burden of disclosure and of being disbelieved.
Partially Accessible
An Access Now rating for places where some parts are barrier-free and others are not. It is the rating this report gives the branch.
