Rights and Law

Why access to a public building is a question of equal benefit of the law.

The Charter

Every person in Canada has an equal right to take part in public life. The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms guarantees equal protection and equal benefit of the law without discrimination based on mental or physical disability (Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, 1982, s. 15(1)).

Rioux (2009, p. 205, as cited in La Monica, 2026, “Introduction to Human Rights and Disability” section, para. 2) adds that equal rights mean each person’s needs matter equally and must guide how societies are planned.

The AODA

Ontario’s Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act names the barriers that keep people out. The barriers that follow in this report are often architectural, informational or policy-based (AODA, 2005, as cited in La Monica, 2026, “Barriers: What are those?” section, para. 1).

What the law does not settle

Postsecondary students with mobility-related disabilities had to disclose a disability to start accommodations, did unseen work to manage fatigue and cost, and met doors, elevators and seating not designed for them (Moritz et al., 2022, pp. 192–197). A right on paper still has to be built into a floor plan.

Key takeaway

Equal benefit of the law is not delivered by a policy document. It is delivered, or not, by doors, elevators and washrooms.
